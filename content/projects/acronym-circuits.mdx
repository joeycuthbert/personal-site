Give GPT-2-small the prompt `"The Tobacco Garage Banana ("` and it reliably completes it with
`"TGB)"`. That's an interesting task for a next-token predictor to be good at, given there's no
"acronym" token anywhere in the input. Thus the model has to notice three nouns, pull the first
letter off each, and assemble them in order. GPT-2's [Indirect Object Identification (IOI)
circuit](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.00593) does something structurally similar by copying a
name from earlier in the sentence to the output via a well-characterized set of attention
heads. This project asks whether the acronym formation described is the same kind of mechanism, using
[TransformerLens](https://github.com/TransformerLensOrg/TransformerLens) on GPT-2-small.

The [full project](https://github.com/joeycuthbert/acronym-recognition) as four experiments
is on GitHub. This page walks through what each one found.

## Data

2000 synthetic examples of the form `(Word1, Word2, Word3) -> Acronym`, generated from a noun
list, e.g. `(Tobacco, Garage, Banana) -> TGB`. Every experiment below runs against a subset of
these with 100-300 examples, chosen for runtime on a CPU.

## How hard is this task for the model?

Before looking for a circuit, it's worth checking the model is actually good at the task.
We do this by forcing the true answer and reading off the probability at each step:

<img src="/acronym-circuits/probability-by-position.png" alt="Bar chart: model's probability of the correct token at each acronym position" style={{maxWidth: "100%"}} />

The first letter is genuinely hard (14% probability), and the remaining two letters as a unit are much harder still (4%), 
and the closing parenthesis is nearly free (57%,
rank ~1). This asymmetry is the reason the experiments below treat "predict the first letter"
and "predict the rest, given the first letter" as two separate sub-tasks rather than one.

## Which heads correlate with the right answer?

Direct logit attribution (DLA) projects each attention head's output onto the
"correct-letter minus competitor-letter direction" in vocab space, using the model's own final
LayerNorm scale. This acts as a correlational measure so a head can score high just because its output
happens to point the right way without the prediction actually depending on it . This is simply a
cheap way to generate candidates.

<img src="/acronym-circuits/dla-per-head.png" alt="Heatmap of DLA logit-difference scores across all layers and heads, for first-letter prediction" style={{maxWidth: "100%"}} />

One head, **L10H10**, stands out sharply above everything else, with L8H11, L9H9, and L11H4
forming a consistent second tier. This ranking held whether the model was predicting the first
letter or the remaining two, and was stable across many random redraws of which competitor
letter to score against (mean logit-diff 3.49 ± 0.12 for the first letter, 3.84 ± 0.18 for the
rest, 95% CI, n=150 each).

## Are those heads necessary?

DLA alone can't distinguish "this head causes the answer" from "this head's output happens to
correlate with it." To check causally, I ran activation patching: run the model once on a clean
prompt and once on a corrupted one, then for every head, splice its *clean* output into the
*corrupted* run and measure how much of the clean-vs-corrupted gap that one substitution
restores.

<img src="/acronym-circuits/activation-patching.png" alt="Heatmap of activation-patching effect by head, first-noun-swap corruption" style={{maxWidth: "100%"}} />

L10H10 alone restores about half the gap (normalized effect ≈0.53) under a first-noun-swap
corruption. Because a causal claim shouldn't rest on one arbitrary choice of corruption, I
also ran it with a second, unrelated corruption (de-capitalizing all three nouns instead of
swapping one). L10H10, L8H11, and L11H4 show up in the top five under *both* corruptions;
L9H9 and a couple of others only show up under one. That partial disagreement is worth taking
seriously rather than only reporting whichever corruption looked cleanest.

I also checked whether any of these heads' output-value (OV) circuit implement a simple "copy the first
letter" function, the way IOI's name-mover heads do? For all four candidate heads, the answer
was no (0% top-5 accuracy). Whatever L10H10 is doing, it isn't a context-independent copy
operation; its causal effect depends on where it actually attends, not just its output weights
in isolation.

## Does an automated circuit reproduce the behavior?

The above is hand-guided: pick heads DLA flagged, check them individually. [Edge Attribution
Patching (EAP)](https://github.com/hannamw/EAP-positional) instead scores every edge in the
model's computation graph automatically and assembles a circuit from the top-scoring ones.
Baseline logit-gain on this dataset was 3.20; the top individual edges by score were exactly
the heads found above (`a10.h10->logits`, `a8.h11->logits`, `a9.h9->logits`,
`a11.h4->logits`), which is a reassuring cross-check on its own.

But the *assembled* 450-edge circuit does not reproduce the behavior: it scores -0.07,
essentially zero, which is roughly -2% of baseline recovered. Individually-important edges do
not compose into a working circuit here, at least not via this thresholding procedure. That
constitutes a negative result. I'm not aware of a good explanation for it yet. A larger
edge budget, a different attribution method, or interaction effects between edges that a
linear attribution score can't see are all plausible next things to try, not things I've
ruled out.

## Where this leaves things

Three independent methods: correlational (DLA), causal (activation patching, under two
different corruptions), and automated (EAP's edge scores) converge on the same small set of
heads, most consistently L10H10. That convergence is the strongest finding here. But the
mechanism doesn't look like IOI's copy circuit (the direct OV-copy test came back negative),
and the automated circuit built from those same important-looking edges doesn't actually work.
Both of those are worth reporting as open questions rather than reporting them as closed results.

## How this compares to prior work

After running these experiments, I found [García-Carrasco et al. (2024), "How does GPT-2 Predict
Acronyms?"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04156) (AISTATS 2024) had already studied the same model
on a closely related task -- three-letter acronyms from real words, versus this project's
four-letter-noun-initial task. Their independently-discovered "letter mover" heads are **8.11,
10.10, 9.9, 11.4** -- exactly the same four heads (L10H10, L8H11, L9H9, L11H4) that DLA,
activation patching, and EAP all converged on above. Thus we have an independent replication of
this project's central finding, reached via a completely different method (their patching setup
vs. this project's DLA + two-corruption patching + EAP).

Two places this project's results diverge from theirs:

- Their circuit is 8 heads, not 4. Beyond the four letter-movers, they identify three
  "previous token heads" (4.11, 1.0, 2.2) that propagate a word's capital letter backward through
  earlier layers, plus one more support head (5.8). This project never tested
  for anything upstream of the movers. This is the most likely explanation for the EAP circuit's failure
  above: a 450-edge circuit assembled without the heads that deliver information to the movers
  wouldn't reproduce the behavior even with the movers themselves correctly identified.
- The OV-copy test asks a different question than their OV analysis. This project tested
  whether each head's OV circuit alone, ignoring what it attends to, implements a
  context-independent copy (it doesn't, for any of the four heads). They analyze the OV circuit
  conditional on attending to the right token, and find a clean copying pattern there. Both can
  be true at once , but theirs is closer to "does this head copy, given correct attention," this
  project's is "does the head hard-code an attention-independent copy in its weights."

Their paper also explains a result this project only observed empirically: first-letter
prediction is hard (14% probability) because, with no earlier word to copy from, it leans on
attention to the BOS token specifically which they verify by ablation, and worth testing
directly against this dataset in a follow-up.

## Limitations

- **Synthetic dataset.** The 2000 examples are auto-generated nonsense noun triples, not
  real-world acronym usage. This characterizes GPT-2's behavior on *this templated task*, not
  "acronym understanding" in general.
- **Small samples, CPU-scale.** Each experiment uses 100-300 of the 2000 rows. Confidence
  intervals are reported for experiments 1-2; experiments 3-4 report point estimates only.
- **The EAP negative result isn't fully explained here.** The likely cause, per
  [García-Carrasco et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04156), is the missing previous-token heads
  -- see *How this compares to prior work*, above.
