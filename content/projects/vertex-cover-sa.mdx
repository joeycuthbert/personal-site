Given a graph $G = (V, E)$, a vertex cover is a subset $C \subseteq V$ such that every edge in
$E$ has at least one endpoint in $C$. The Minimum Vertex Cover (MVC) problem asks for the
smallest such $C$. It's an NP-hard problem, so exact branch-and-bound solvers pay for optimality with
runtime while local-search algorithms like NuMVC (Cai, Su, Luo & Sattar, *JAIR* 2013) trade
exactness for speed. NuSA takes NuMVC and asks: does adding simulated
annealing to it help?

The full project can be found [here](https://github.com/joeycuthbert/NuSA). Here we will walk through
what it found.  

TLDR: Annealing one part of NuMVC does nothing, but
annealing a different part helps a great deal, and the reason it helps has nothing to do with
annealing.

**Introduction.** This project began as a project for CSE6140 (Algorithms) at Georgia Tech. I had previous experience with simulated annealing while taking
ISyE6645 (Monte Carlo Methods) the semester prior, and it sprang to mind when I had to implement a local-search algorithm for MVC as part of an end of class project. 
The course provided some (fairly easy)
benchmarks to test our algorithms on and NuSA performed incredibly well on them. This led me to testing the algorithm further on a few DIMACS benchmarks and comparing against the original NuMVC
results. Unfortunately, NuSA did not live up to the expectations I had set, and consistently failed on some of the smallest DIMACS graphs which NuMVC solved fairly quickly. This led me to attempt
to find out if simulated annealing was helping the algorithm at all. 

## How NuMVC searches

NuMVC alternates removing a vertex from the current cover and adding one back to repair whatever
edges that removal uncovered, guided by a `dscore` heuristic (how many edges a vertex's removal
would uncover) and an edge-weighting scheme that raises the cost of leaving stubborn edges
uncovered over time. Unlike other MVC algorithms, the choice between which vertex to remove and subsequently add are independent. 
Whenever it finds a feasible cover of size $k$, it deterministically drops
the cheapest vertex and tries for $k - 1$. The question I wanted to answer was whether
classic simulated annealing adds anything on top, tried in two different places.

## Finding 1: annealing the removal step does nothing

The obvious place to try annealing is the removal decision itself. Instead of always removing
the best-`dscore` vertex, accept a worse one with probability $\exp(\Delta / T)$. Tested on C550.9 with 10 seeds, 12s
cutoff, target 443 (the optimum):

| config | success | mean time to target | acceptance |
|---|---|---|---|
| default (T=3.0, shipped schedule) | 10/10 | 0.337s | 0.01-0.46 (rises as it cools) |
| descent (T≈0) | 10/10 | 0.215s | 0.01 |
| warm (constant T=2.0) | 9/10 | 2.818s | 0.36 |
| hot (constant T=10) | **0/10** | never (best found: 444) | 0.82 |

We see near-zero temperature is at least as good as the tuned default. Interestingly, raising
the temperature slows the search down, and in the extreme case at T=10 it never reaches the optimum at all
inside the cutoff. The likely reason is that NuMVC's edge-weighting scheme is already a
good enough diversification mechanism. Undirected noise on the same decision competes with that guidance
rather than adding to it.

## Finding 2: perturbing the cover level has a large effect

We inspect a different intervention by occasionally adding a
vertex without removing one, so the search is forced off a level it's stuck
on. On C1000.9 with 60 runs per arm, 30s cutoff, and target 932 (best known size):

| arm                | median level-ups | success |   RMST | HR vs. baseline   |
|:-------------------|-----------------:|--------:|-------:|:------------------|
| A: no perturbation |                0 |   28/60 | 21.56s | --                |
| B: rate ~1/32000   |              213 |   41/60 | 15.63s | 2.02 [1.25, 3.27] |
| C: rate ~1/2500    |              966 |   57/60 |  8.43s | 5.21 [3.31, 8.19] |
| D: rate ~1/263     |            9,522 |   59/60 |  7.64s | 5.95 [3.79, 9.33] |

<img src="/vertex-cover/km-survival.png" alt="Kaplan-Meier survival curves for time to reach cover size 932, four arms, C1000.9" style={{maxWidth: "100%"}} />


We see from the plot that the effect saturates. Arms C and D both beat the baseline (A)
([log-rank](https://metricgate.com/docs/log-rank-test) $p < 10^{-6}$) but they do not
beat each other. Comparing D vs. C gives a
[hazard ratio](https://metricgate.com/docs/hazard-ratio-calculator-from-km) of 1.14
with $p = 0.55$, which means the difference between the two arms is negligible. Thus going from zero
perturbations to roughly a thousand buys nearly all of the speedup and a further
10x in rate buys nothing measurable.

## Finding 3: the annealing schedule on that perturbation was inert

The perturbation fires on a patience counter: after `patience` iterations at the same level,
fire with probability $\exp(-1/T)$ each subsequent iteration. The expected interval between
perturbations is `patience + 1/p`, and temperature only controls the second term. At
`patience = 2000`, cooling $T$ from 2.0 to 0.5 changes the firing rate by **0.3%**, thus the
patience gate dominates completely. Thus the annealing schedule was negligible in both configurations, since for most of the run-time the annealing is not even turned on.

## Why survival analysis?

Time-to-target data under a fixed cutoff is right-censored. In other words, a run that didn't reach the target
didn't necessarily "fail", it just hadn't reached it yet when the budget ran out. Comparing success rates
alone throws away timing information from the runs that succeeded, and comparing mean
time-to-target throws away the runs that missed. The log-rank test, exponential hazard ratio, and restricted mean
survival time (RMST) above are standard tools for this analysis since they use every run, whether it was a hit or
miss. 

## Conclusion

We are left with some positive and negative results. On one hand the algorithm that got me a near perfect score on my graduate algorithms final project was in reality far worse than the algorithm it drew inspiration from, and
attempted to build off of. Rather than be discouraged from this negative result, I was able to build tests that helped explain why it failed, and subsequently look for another improvement on the algorithm such as perturbing the solution on a 
given schedule. Yet, this still doesn't close the gap in comparison to NuMVC. A controlled,
same-hardware comparison would be the most useful comparison.
