Given a graph $G = (V, E)$, a vertex cover is a subset $C \subseteq V$ such that every edge in
$E$ has at least one endpoint in $C$. The Minimum Vertex Cover (MVC) problem asks for the
smallest such $C$. It's an NP-hard problem, so exact branch-and-bound solvers pay for optimality with
runtime while local-search algorithms like NuMVC (Cai, Su, Luo & Sattar, *JAIR* 2013) trade
exactness for speed. NuSA takes NuMVC and asks: does adding simulated
annealing to it help?

The full project can be found [here](https://github.com/joeycuthbert/NuSA). Here we will walk through
what it found.  

TLDR: Annealing one part of NuMVC does nothing, but
annealing a different part helps a great deal, and the reason it helps has nothing to do with
annealing.

**Introduction.** This is not state of the art algorithm. This project began as a project for CSE6140 (Algorithms) at Georgia Tech. I had previous experience with simulated annealing while taking
ISyE6645 (Monte Carlo Methods) the semester prior, and it sprang to mind when I had to implement a local-search algorithm for MVC as part of the project. The course provided some (fairly easy)
benchmarks to test our algorithms on and NuSA performed incredibly well on them. This led me to testing the algorithm further on a few DIMACS benchmarks and comparing against the original NuMVC
results. Unfortunately, NuSA did not live up to the expectations I had set, and consistently failed on some of the smallest DIMACS graphs which NuMVC solved fairly quickly. This led me to attempt
to find out if simulated annealing was helping the algorithm at all. 

## How NuMVC searches

NuMVC alternates removing a vertex from the current cover and adding one back to repair whatever
edges that removal uncovered, guided by a `dscore` heuristic (how many edges a vertex's removal
would uncover) and an edge-weighting scheme that raises the cost of leaving stubborn edges
uncovered over time. Whenever it finds a feasible cover of size $k$, it deterministically drops
the cheapest vertex and tries for $k - 1$. The question I wanted to answer was whether
classic simulated annealing adds anything on top, tried in two different places.

## Finding 1: annealing the removal step does nothing

The obvious place to try annealing is the removal decision itself. Instead of always removing
the best-`dscore` vertex, accept a worse one with probability $\exp(\Delta / T)$. Tested directly
on `data/C500.txt` -- the real C500.9 complement, not a synthetic stand-in -- 10 seeds, 12s
cutoff, target 443 (the optimum):

| config | success | mean time to target | acceptance |
|---|---|---|---|
| default (T=3.0, shipped schedule) | 10/10 | 0.337s | 0.01-0.46 (rises as it cools) |
| descent (T≈0) | 10/10 | 0.215s | 0.01 |
| warm (constant T=2.0) | 9/10 | 2.818s | 0.36 |
| hot (constant T=10) | **0/10** | never (best found: 444, one above optimum) | 0.82 |

Near-zero temperature is at least as good as the tuned default -- slightly faster, here. Raising
the temperature doesn't just slow the search down: at T=10 it never reaches the optimum at all
inside the cutoff. The likely reason, same as before: NuMVC's edge-weighting scheme is already a
targeted, memory-based diversification mechanism, raising the cost of a configuration until it
becomes untenable. Undirected Metropolis noise on the same decision competes with that guidance
rather than adding to it.

## Finding 2: perturbing the cover level has a large effect

A different intervention: instead of touching which vertex gets removed, occasionally *add* a
vertex without removing one, so the search is forced off a level (a fixed cover size) it's stuck
on. On C1000.9 (60 runs per arm, 30s cutoff, target 932 -- the best known size, not proven
optimal, see the NuMVC comparison below):

| arm | median level-ups | success | RMST | HR vs. baseline |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: no perturbation | 0 | 28/60 | 21.56s | -- |
| B: rate ~1/32000 | 213 | 41/60 | 15.63s | 2.02 [1.25, 3.27] |
| C: rate ~1/2500 | 966 | 57/60 | 8.43s | 5.21 [3.31, 8.19] |
| D: rate ~1/263 | 9,522 | 59/60 | 7.64s | 5.95 [3.79, 9.33] |

Log-rank $p < 10^{-6}$ for arms C and D against A. D vs. C is *not* significant (HR 1.14,
p = 0.55) -- the effect saturates. Going from zero to roughly a thousand perturbations buys
nearly everything; a further 10x in rate buys nothing measurable. On the smaller C500.9 instance
(25 runs, 30s cutoff, target 443, the optimum), the level-perturbation arm reaches 25/25 with a
mean time-to-target of 0.445s -- no baseline log for that instance survives in the record, so
that comparison isn't reported here.

## Finding 3: the annealing schedule on that perturbation was inert

The perturbation fires on a patience counter: after `patience` iterations at the same level,
fire with probability $\exp(-1/T)$ each subsequent iteration. The expected interval between
perturbations is `patience + 1/p`, and temperature only controls the second term. At
`patience = 2000`, cooling $T$ from 2.0 to 0.5 changes the firing rate by **0.3%** -- the
patience gate dominates completely, and below $T \approx 0.13$ the rate collapses to zero rather
than declining gradually. The schedule was never capable of modulating anything, in either
configuration tested. A related trap: a schedule specified in *iterations* silently rescales
when the iteration budget changes -- one tuned on 150k-iteration runs was effectively switched
off for 94% of a 12M-iteration run.

## Comparison to the original NuMVC

This is the comparison I mentioned above -- the one that told me NuSA wasn't living up to
reference NuMVC on some of these instances. Cai, Su, Luo & Sattar (2013), Table 1, report NuMVC's
performance on these same four instances: 100 independent runs each, 2000s cutoff, on a 3GHz
Intel Core 2 Duo E8400 with 4GB RAM.

| instance | \|V\| | best-known VC size | proven optimal? | success | avg time | avg steps |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C125.9 | 125 | 91 | yes | 100/100 | &lt;0.001s | 136 |
| C250.9 | 250 | 206 | yes | 100/100 | &lt;0.001s | 3,256 |
| C500.9 | 500 | 443 | yes | 100/100 | 0.128s | 133,595 |
| C1000.9 | 1000 | 932 | **no** -- best known only | 100/100 | 2.020s | 1,154,155 |

(That's also why C1000.9's target above is described as "best known" rather than "optimal" --
the paper marks only 91/206/443 with an asterisk for proven optimality.)

This isn't apples-to-apples: different hardware (a 2008-era Core 2 Duo vs. whatever ran the logs
in the repo, almost certainly faster per core), a 2000s cutoff instead of 30s, and their C++
implementation instead of this project's C port. With all of that granted, the gap is still large.
Reference NuMVC reaches C1000.9's target in 100/100 runs, averaging 2.02s. This project's *best*
arm on that instance (D, the constant-temperature level perturbation above) reaches it in 59/60
runs with a 3.8x larger RMST (7.64s vs. 2.02s), and the no-perturbation baseline manages only
28/60. On the smaller C500.9, the gap is narrower in kind if not in size -- reference NuMVC
averages 0.128s; this project's own removal-step-ablation default arm above (a different
experiment, same instance) averages 0.337s at 10/10, roughly 2.6x slower, same order of
magnitude. The gap widening with instance size rather than staying flat suggests whatever
reference NuMVC does better matters more as the graph gets harder, not less -- worth digging into,
not just noting.

## Why survival analysis, not just success rate

Time-to-target data under a fixed cutoff is right-censored: a run that didn't reach the target
didn't "fail," it just hadn't reached it yet when the budget ran out. Comparing success rates
alone throws away timing information from the runs that succeeded; comparing mean
time-to-target alone throws away the runs that missed, understating how much worse a weak arm
really is. The log-rank test, exponential hazard ratio, and bootstrap-CI restricted mean
survival time (RMST) above are standard tools for exactly this -- they use every run, hit or
miss. `analysis/analyze.py` in the repo implements all of it against the raw solver logs, using
only the Python standard library.

## A correctness bug along the way

The original incremental `dscore` update negated incorrectly on flip: it added the edge weight
inside the neighbor loop, landing on 0 instead of the correct `+S`. Every touched vertex ended
up with `dscore == 0` -- the maximum possible value for a cover vertex -- so the search
preferentially re-removed vertices it had just added. The fix is a single negation applied
outside the loop. Both implementations now carry a debug mode that recomputes all scores from
scratch and asserts they match the incremental values.

## Where this leaves things

A negative result and a positive one, and neither is subtle: undirected annealing noise on the
removal step is dead weight next to NuMVC's existing edge-weighting mechanism, while a simple,
un-annealed perturbation on cover size cuts median time-to-optimum by roughly 3x on the hardest
instance tested. Neither closes the gap to reference NuMVC, though -- even this project's best
configuration is still meaningfully behind published NuMVC on the same instances. A controlled,
same-hardware comparison (not just published numbers next to my own) is the most useful thing
left to do here -- see the repo's README for the full future-work list.
